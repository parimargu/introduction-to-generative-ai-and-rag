Three days in Madurai turned out to be one of the most memorable trips I have taken in Tamil Nadu. I arrived by the overnight Pandian Express from Chennai Egmore, which left at 9:40 PM and reached Madurai Junction at around 5:15 AM. The sleeper coach was clean and the ticket cost about 400 rupees. From the station it was a ten-minute auto ride to my hotel near the West Masi Street. Booking the train three weeks in advance on the IRCTC app was essential because it fills up quickly during festival season. The Meenakshi Amman Temple is the heart of the city and the main reason most visitors come here. The temple complex has 14 gopurams, the tallest being the southern tower at about 52 metres. Every tower is covered with thousands of brightly painted sculptures of gods, goddesses and mythical creatures. Legend says the temple celebrates the wedding of Goddess Meenakshi to Lord Sundareswarar, a form of Shiva. The Hall of a Thousand Pillars inside the temple actually has 985 carved pillars and now houses a small art museum. Mobile phones are not allowed inside, so I left mine at the counter near the east gate for a small fee. Then there was the food, which honestly deserves its own trip. Madurai is famous for jigarthanda, a cold drink made from milk, almond gum, sarsaparilla syrup and ice cream. The most popular shop for it is Famous Jigarthanda on Vilakkuthoon Road, where a glass costs around 60 rupees. For breakfast I had soft idlis with five kinds of chutney at a small mess that opens at 6 AM. Kari dosa, a thick dosa topped with spiced minced mutton and egg, is the city's signature dinner dish. Most street food stalls stay open until well past midnight, which is why Madurai is called Thoonga Nagaram, the city that never sleeps. The weather, however, was a real challenge. I visited in late April, when daytime temperatures reached 39 degrees Celsius. The humidity made it feel even hotter by early afternoon. Locals told me the best months to visit are November to February, when the temperature stays between 20 and 30 degrees. If you do visit in summer, plan outdoor sightseeing before 11 AM and after 4 PM, and carry at least two litres of water. On the last day I explored the history of the city at Thirumalai Nayakkar Mahal. This palace was built in 1636 by King Thirumalai Nayak of the Nayak dynasty. Its architecture blends Dravidian and Islamic styles, with huge white pillars about 20 metres tall. Only about a quarter of the original palace survives today; the rest was dismantled by the king's grandson. Every evening there is a sound and light show in English at 6:45 PM that tells the story of the Nayak kings. Overall, I spent about 6,500 rupees for three days including train tickets, a budget hotel at 1,400 rupees per night, food and entry fees. Madurai is very affordable for budget travellers. Auto-rickshaws are the easiest way to move around, and most rides inside the old city cost between 50 and 100 rupees. I would happily return for the Chithirai festival in April, when the city celebrates the celestial wedding with huge processions.
